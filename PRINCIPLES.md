# Principles

This library did not start as a collection of prompts. It started as a set of motion-design opinions, argued over while building real pages, and only later hardened into a prompt format. The principles came first; the seven-part prompt format ([FORMAT.md](FORMAT.md)) is the mechanism that forces a generated page to obey them; every prompt here was then tested by actually generating a page from it and checking the result.

The eight sections below are the decisions that survived that process. Each explains why the rule exists and what breaks when you ignore it.

## 1. Emotion first

Every prompt in this library names an emotion before it names a single easing curve or duration — the format forbids writing motion parameters until the emotion is fixed. That ordering is the whole method. An emotion implies a path shape: joy moves in curves and upward arcs, calm in gentle sine waves, urgency in straight lines. The path then implies the easing — a curve wants an overshoot, a straight line wants a clean ease-out — and duration follows last.

Reverse the chain and the system collapses. Start with "use ease-out-back, 300ms" and you have picked a feeling without knowing it: the bounce in ease-out-back *is* joy, whether the page is selling a summer camp or a tax dashboard. Do that across a whole site and every page moves the same way, because the same default easings keep winning. The emotion table in FORMAT.md is a working convention we chose, not a laboratory finding. What we verified is the failure mode: prompts that led with parameters produced pages whose motion contradicted their content — a bouncy pricing toggle on a premium page, a dead-linear reveal on a children's page. The table is opinion, stated as opinion; the cost of getting the order wrong is the part we actually saw.

## 2. One persona per page

A persona here is not a mood board. It is a binding commitment: a duration band, a signature easing, and an overshoot range, applied consistently to everything on the page. Playful means 150–300ms, ease-out-back, 10–20% overshoot. Premium means 350–600ms, a (0.4, 0, 0.2, 1) curve, zero overshoot. When a page commits to one persona, a user learns its timing within the first interaction and can predict the next one.

Mix personas and the commitment dissolves. A hero that bounces in Playful and then hands the user to a Premium pricing section does not feel like two moods; it feels like the page changed its mind. The timing language the user just learned is now wrong, and every subsequent animation has to be re-read from scratch. There is one legitimate downgrade: a high-energy persona can own the first screen while a quieter one runs the rest. An Energetic hero with a Corporate body works because the first screen is a promise and the body is the delivery. The reverse does not work — a Corporate hero followed by an Energetic pricing section feels like the page suddenly shouting. Energy can step down, never up, and it steps down once.

## 3. Three layers

Every prompt requires three layers of motion: a primary layer that carries the meaning, a secondary layer at 30–50% of the primary's amplitude, and an ambient layer at 10–20%. This is the rule most people skip first, because the primary layer alone already looks "done" in a screenshot. But motion is not judged in screenshots. It is judged over seconds, and a page with only a primary layer feels like a switch: things arrive, then nothing moves.

The two quieter layers solve different problems. The secondary layer — a balloon drifting at half the hero's amplitude, a feature list arriving 100ms after its card — gives the primary motion something to be *relative to*. Without it, a 300ms entrance has no scale; the user cannot tell whether it was fast or slow, because nothing else was moving at a different speed. The ambient layer — clouds drifting over eight seconds, a background glow breathing at 3% opacity — keeps the page alive between interactions. It is deliberately too slow and too faint to be noticed directly, which is exactly why it works: the page feels inhabited rather than frozen, without ever competing for attention. Skip the layers and you do not get a simpler page — you get one that alternates between dead and startling, the most common failure we saw in generated pages before this rule was added. A single layer is a flat image that occasionally jumps.

## 4. The 1/3 rule

No single motion in this library travels more than one third of the screen — and when three or more elements move at once, the same cap applies to the group. The number is a judgement call, but the reasoning behind it is not ours: it is the limit of what a viewer's eyes can comfortably track. A full-screen slide forces the eye to chase the element across the whole viewport, re-fixate, and then re-read the layout it left behind.

Cross the cap and three costs arrive together. The first is physical: large, fast, full-screen movement is the classic trigger for motion sickness, and it is worst for the people who can least afford it — users with vestibular sensitivity, who will close the tab rather than file a complaint. The second is attentional: a long travel distance steals focus from whatever the motion was supposed to point at, because the journey itself becomes the event. The third is cumulative: one full-screen entrance is dramatic, five in a row are exhausting, and a page that exhausts its visitor does not get its call-to-action read. We chose 1/3 because it is the largest distance that still reads as "the element moved within my field of view" on both a phone and a desktop: 130px on a 390px phone, 480px on a 1440px desktop.

## 5. Transform + opacity only

The format allows exactly two animated properties: `transform` and `opacity`. Everything else — width, height, margins, shadows, filters — is banned from animation, and the reason is the browser's rendering pipeline. Transform and opacity changes can be handled on the GPU compositor thread: the browser paints the element once into a layer and then moves or fades that layer, without re-laying-out the page or repainting pixels. Animate `width` instead and the browser must recalculate the layout of everything the element touches, repaint the affected area, and re-composite.

The cost is not theoretical. A `width` animation on a card forces layout work for the card, its siblings, and anything positioned relative to it; a `box-shadow` animation forces a repaint of the entire shadow region each frame; a `filter: blur()` animation is among the most expensive operations a browser can be asked to do repeatedly. On a fast desktop the result is a dropped frame or two that nobody notices. On a mid-range phone — where most vibe-coded pages are actually read — it is visible stutter, and stutter is the one motion failure users notice instantly, because it reads as broken rather than merely ugly. Restricting the vocabulary also forces better design: when you cannot animate width, you reach for `scaleX` or `translateX` instead, and those almost always look better — a scale keeps proportions honest, a translate keeps the layout footprint stable so nothing else on the page jumps.

## 6. No linear easing for spatial motion

Nothing in the physical world moves at a constant speed. A ball thrown upward decelerates, a door swings fastest in the middle, a person standing up accelerates out of the chair and then settles. Linear easing — the same speed from start to finish — matches no physical profile at all, so the brain reads it as artificial: not wrong, exactly, but robotic, like a conveyor belt rather than a thing moving.

That is why the format bans linear easing for any spatial motion and reserves it for the three cases where constant speed is the *honest* representation: spinners, progress bars, and timers. A spinner is not pretending to be a physical object travelling through space; it is a clock, and a clock should tick evenly. A progress bar is a measurement, and a measurement that speeds up and slows down is lying about the progress. The distinction is between motion that represents a thing moving and motion that represents time passing — linear is correct for the second and wrong for the first. Violate this and the failure is subtle but total: a linearly-moving card does not look broken in a screenshot and does not jank or stutter; it just feels cheap in a way the viewer cannot articulate. That is the worst kind of defect for a prompt library, because the user who generated the page cannot see it either — they asked for "smooth," the model delivered mathematically smooth, and the result feels like a machine.

## 7. Test every prompt

A well-written prompt is not a guarantee. It is a request, and the model that receives it is free to misunderstand it, half-follow it, or follow the letter while missing the intent. That is why every prompt in this library was tested the same way: generate a real single-file page from it, run a 13-item structure check, render it in a headless browser and look at the screenshots, verify the interactions by actually performing them, and check it at 390px.

Each of the four layers catches a different class of failure, and none of them is redundant. The structure check catches the model quietly dropping a requirement: a missing secondary layer, a duration outside the persona's band, an ease that drifted from ease-out-back to ease-out. The screenshot catches what the structure check cannot — a layout that is technically correct and visually wrong, a hero that overflows, a colour that reads as muddy rather than premium. Interaction verification catches the gap between "the code contains a handler" and "the handler works": a pricing toggle that flips the label but not the price, a cube that drags but never snaps. Skip the testing and you are shipping a library of intentions. We know this because we nearly did: an early version of the pricing prompt passed its structure check while its reveal animation was silently overriding the recommended tier's static 8px lift — a bug invisible to every check except the screenshot and the interaction test, both of which caught it. A prompt that has never been run is a hypothesis. A prompt that has generated a page, been screenshotted, and had its toggle clicked is a fact.

## 8. Where the persona system comes from

The four personas did not come from a design textbook. They came from the opposite direction: we built pages, noticed that the same four timing personalities kept recurring, and named them. Playful is the timing of things meant to delight — short durations, a bounce with 10–20% overshoot, the feel of a toy. Premium is the timing of things meant to be admired — slow, 350–600ms, a standard curve with zero overshoot. Corporate is the timing of things meant to be used — 200–400ms, clean, at most 3% overshoot. Energetic is the timing of things meant to be noticed — 100–250ms, ease-out-expo, up to 30% overshoot.

Each persona has a boundary, and the boundary is where the judgement lives. Playful crosses the line the moment the content stops being fun: a bouncy error message, a springy "payment failed" state, a cheerful overshoot on a cancellation confirmation. The motion is still well-executed; it is just telling the user to feel delighted about something they are not delighted about, and that mismatch reads as the product not understanding the moment. Energetic is the persona we warn about most, because its failure mode is accumulation, not mismatch: one fast, punchy entrance is exciting, six of them are a strobe light, and the page that shouts everywhere is a page whose user has learned to ignore it. It belongs on launch and event pages, briefly, and then it should get out of the way.

Corporate and Premium are the pair people confuse, because both are restrained and both avoid overshoot. The difference is not colour or font — it is intent. Corporate motion exists to get out of the user's way: fast enough to be unnoticed, its success measured by how little attention it draws. Premium motion exists to *be* noticed, slowly and deliberately: a 600ms title entrance is the page asking you to look at it. Same restraint, opposite goals. Put Corporate timing on a brand film and it feels rushed; put Premium timing on a settings panel and it feels like the software is showing off.

## What this library is not

It is not an effects collection. An effects collection optimises for variety — the more transitions, the better the demo. This library optimises for the opposite: a small set of motions, each with a reason, applied consistently enough that a page feels like one thing. If you want a page with twelve different entrance animations, this is the wrong repo.

It is also not a copy of MotionSites. MotionSites is a catalogue of prompts other people wrote, in whatever format they happened to use. But a catalogue cannot enforce a method, because it has no method — every prompt is a different shape, and the reader has to reverse-engineer the thinking from the output. This library is the method first and the prompts second: one format, four personas, one emotion table, one set of iron rules, and every prompt is an instance of those rather than an independent invention.

Finally, the prompts split into two jobs, and the split is deliberate. The basic prompts cover a single pattern — one hero, one parallax, one pricing section — and their job is to get that pattern right in isolation. The advanced prompts cover composite timing: several patterns on one page, sequenced, with the layers and the persona held consistent across all of them. The basics teach the model what good motion looks like; the advanced ones teach it how to keep that quality when the page gets complicated. The advanced prompts exist because a page that is five correct patterns in a row can still feel incoherent, and coherence is the thing no single-pattern prompt can deliver.
